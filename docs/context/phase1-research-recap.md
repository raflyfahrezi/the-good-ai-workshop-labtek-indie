# Phase 1 Research: How Newsrooms Work and Where AI Is Currently Used

This is the report every journalist in the room will have been briefed on. The 7 pain points they pick from come straight from here. Knowing the quotes and personas lets me speak their language.

## The opening quotes (sets the mood)

- "Apakah itu meringankan beban jurnalis? Enggak, malah kayaknya (berpotensi) mengeksploitasi jurnalis." (Iman Herdiana, Editor, Bandung Bergerak)
- "AI itu kan biasanya supaya kerjanya lebih cepat... Nggak juga, sama aja kerjanya." (Mawa Kresna, Editor, Project Multatuli)

The report's own reading: journalists have real concerns, both about exploitation and about efficiency they haven't felt yet. AI has to be efficient **and** on the side of journalists' needs and wellbeing.

**Takeaway for me:** walk in skeptical-friendly, not as an AI salesman. The room has heard "AI makes you faster" and doesn't believe it.

## Newsrooms studied (7)

Bandung Bergerak, Digital Mama, AyoBandung, Ekuatorial, Tirto.id, Project Multatuli, Mongabay (Indonesia).

Picked to contrast on three lenses:

| Lens | Contrast | What they wanted to learn |
|---|---|---|
| Organisational structure | Small (<15 core editorial) vs medium | How responsibilities and operational work are distributed |
| Production model | Fast-cycle (flash news) vs long-form (in-depth, investigative) | How speed, coordination, research, verification differ |
| Audience model | Conventional (one-way) vs participatory (two-way, community) | How audience engagement changes post-production work |

Three perspectives studied: Journalist to Public (discovery, sourcing, verification), Journalist to Newsroom (assignment, review, handoffs, publishing, distribution), Newsroom Management (contributors, admin, partnerships, sustainability).

## Five key findings (executive summary)

1. **Frictions in the workflow.** Accessing sources, verifying evidence, keeping context through interruptions, adapting stories for platforms.
2. **Responsibility and judgment.** Journalists focus on stories. Editors, senior staff and leaders absorb coordination, admin, fundraising.
3. **AI use and readiness.** AI already used for research, transcription, summarisation, translation, data processing, even drafting and repackaging. But **individually**, allowed to an extent, not supported by the newsroom.
4. **AI shifts workload to later stages.** Faster output doesn't mean less total work. Errors, missing context and unclear AI use push the burden to verification, rewriting and editorial review.
5. **Little value in return.** AI platforms benefit from newsroom content. Newsrooms get little traffic, revenue or audience relationship back.

**Implication:** Responsible AI should **reduce total workload across roles and stages**, keep outputs **linked to original sources**, stay **subject to human editorial judgment**, and be something the newsroom can **govern and maintain**.

## The editorial workflow (8 stages)

**Plan → Research → Report → Draft → Review → Publish → Distribute → Post-publish**

- Same stages across every newsroom, regardless of size or rhythm.
- It's **not linear**: new info changes the angle, review sends a story back for more reporting, verification sends it back to production, corrections reopen it after publishing.
- Two modes, often done by the **same journalist at the same time**:
  - **Regular-cycle**: assigned early, each stage scoped to the publication window, mostly forward.
  - **Extended / in-depth**: assignment may follow exploration, stages expand, overlap and repeat as findings reshape the story.

Role verbs per stage (from the report's role map):

| Stage | Journalist | Editor |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | Pitch | Assign |
| Research | Map | Frame |
| Report | Gather | Guide |
| Draft | Build | Consult |
| Review | Revise | Review |
| Publish | Finalize | Approve |
| Distribute | Support | Support |
| Post-publish | Engage | Evaluate |

Plus **non-editorial activities** around it, which land mostly on editors and leaders: coordinating contributors, fellowship programs, interview letters, events (editors), and fundraising, budgets, contracts, donors, clients, partners, strategy (leaders).

## Personas (3)

### Faris, 25, "The Conventional" (junior journalist, part-time editor)
- Small newsroom (<15 people), everyone does everything. Fresh out of college.
- Goals: accurate impactful investigative/environmental stories, verify fast, grow skills without org support, not burn out.
- Frustrations: unclear roles, editing duties pile on, late hours, uses many free AI tools because newsroom won't pay, accepts overload as normal.
- AI: ChatGPT and Gemini (brainstorm, draft), TurboScribe (transcription), translation, AI detection, data summarisation, typo checks.
- New risk: **worry about data security on free tools for sensitive work**, accuracy anxiety, **double-checks all AI output**.
- AI touchpoint: personal adoption, zero institutional support, all free plans.

### Galih / Iskandar, 33, "The Coder" (mainly an editor, de facto tech lead)
- 10+ years experience. Experiments with AI because nobody else does. Active in journalism-tech communities (**this is basically the Journocoders profile, i.e. my Session 1 crowd**). Still writes data stories.
- Goals: help colleagues do data-informed stories, efficient editing without compromising ethics, **protect sensitive data sovereignty while still using AI**.
- Frustrations: when journalists use AI without disclosure, **editing workload doubles**. Lack of transparency means verification nightmares. Hard to scale innovations across the org. Speed vs integrity.
- AI: **Codex for building custom newsroom apps**, ChatGPT and Gemini for advanced workflows, transcription/translation, AI detection, **custom agentic AI experiments**, data summarisation.
- New challenge: weekly time learning AI, builds internal tools and docs for colleagues, advocates for policies and subscriptions, tracks how AI invisibly redistributes work.
- AI touchpoint: **highest of the three**. Expect this person in my team to be technical. I can go one level deeper with them.

### Galuh / Ratih, 48, "The Glue" (Executive Director, jill-of-all-trades)
- Meetings with funders, mentoring, partnerships, audience growth. Health suffers from stress.
- Goals: keep the newsroom alive, protect editorial independence while chasing revenue, nurture next-gen journalists, navigate Big Tech crawling policies and collective bargaining with platforms.
- Frustrations: revenue never stops being the problem, "the distribution bottleneck is where journalism dies", traffic collapsing while Big Tech profits, admin still manual, AI use individual and strategically unresolved.
- AI: ChatGPT for emails and funding proposals, delegates AI exploration to the most tech-savvy person then reviews, makes strategic calls on content protection (crawling).
- AI touchpoint: low-moderate. Understands impact, no bandwidth.

### 1. Information discovery & sense-making
Fragmented info (gov portals, company records, reports, regulation, past coverage) → evaluate (verify quality and recency, recover original context, assess citability) → connect the dots (what's known, how it connects, what needs verification).
- "I use AI to parse the data and extract the tables. Once I have the data, I analyze the data myself in a spreadsheet." (Kresna, PM)
- "We start by researching the initial data and mapping out the sources... we still have to determine what is relevant and what isn't." (Yulia, Mongabay)

### 2. Source access, safety & emotional judgment
Access depends on trust (availability, language, networks, past media experience). Reaching sources is physically demanding (forest, rainy season, crowds, no signal). Sensitive interviews need judgment (probe vs empathy, protect vulnerable sources).
- "I just asked her... she answered me with an angry tone. She's wary of journalists." (Insan, BB)
- "...going through forest... rainy season... slippery rocks and hills." (Yulia, Mongabay)
- "I have to offer them protection, for example, by offering whether to use their real name or a pseudonym." (Nizar, Tirto)

### 3. Transcription inaccuracies
Noise, overlapping speakers, mixed languages, similar-sounding sources distort transcripts. Text loses tone, emphasis, emotion. Journalists go back to the recording to verify quotes. Transcripts still save time by **helping locate quotes**.
- "Sometimes it's about the tone, the vocal emphasis, what did they actually mean? I want to capture the emotion." (Insan, BB)
- "Sometimes the words aren't clear... So I have to listen again." (Alin, Digital Mama)
- "Before AI, I used to transcribe everything myself so I could remember it all. It took even longer than the interview itself." (Nizar, Tirto)

### 4. Interruptions disturb reporting continuity
Context accumulates across research, interviews, documents, recordings, notes. Regular assignments on totally different topics break the flow. On return: reopen materials, recall earlier reasoning, recheck what's missing.
- "I store all recordings, transcripts, documentation, data I found, notes from the field, all of it there including maps... including the writing." (Yulia, Mongabay)
- "...you're in serious mode, focused, then you have to switch... Getting back into it, the mood, the concentration, takes effort." (Yulia, Mongabay)

### 5. Lack of AI governance (out of scope, P009)
- Generally accepted: supporting tasks (research, brainstorm, transcription, reading docs, data).
- Grey area: assisting with drafting (OK if journalist reviews, reshapes, takes responsibility).
- Problematic: submitting AI output as-is. Unedited output is "empty, repetitive, overly polished". Repair becomes the editor's job.
- "AI is fine for transcripts, translation, processing large data, but the final product has to be written by a human." (Tri Joko, EIC, BB)
- "It's okay for them to use GPT, but it has to be humanized." (Lusia, Mongabay)
- "Their writing used to be rough, then suddenly became very polished, but empty..." (Kresna, PM)

Even though out of scope, **disclosure/traceability** shows up as a design requirement in every prototype.

### 6. Audience & news distribution
Attention spread across formats (reels, carousels, newsletters, channel posts). Distribution became a second editorial workflow (select, compress, reframe). Social reach doesn't bring readers to the website.
- "...survive in an era when audiences increasingly prefer audio and video." (Hengky, AyoBandung)
- "For a single issue, we distribute... long-form stories, short documentaries, Reels and carousels." (Asep, EIC Ekuatorial)
- "People on Instagram just follow the trends... they don't move over and become traffic for us." (Fira, Business Director, Ekuatorial)

### 7. Revenue continuity & value capture
Revenue unstable (Ekuatorial can't rely on grants anymore, Project Multatuli membership is under 5% of revenue and hard to grow). Each income source adds work (proposals, budgets, contracts, reports, meetings). Platforms and AI use content without visits, ownership or compensation.
- "There's no ideal business model for media anymore... Media are shutting down." (Ekuatorial EIC)
- "Writing proposals, making budgets, signing contracts, and so many meetings." (Evi, Director, PM)
- "AI easily takes what's ours... charity work. So that AI can learn and get smarter." (EIC, BB)

### 8. Operational & administrative load
Coordinating roles stretch beyond formal duties. Invoices, receipts, reporting advances, contributor payments, membership records collected and checked by hand. When systems don't fit, coordinators absorb the work.
- "Collecting invoices and receipts, it still has to be done manually. The AI can't just go and chase invoices from person A, person B, person C." (Evi, PM)
- "If the fellows had to get into Asana, it would just create more hassle... So I enter the workflow myself." (Lusia, Mongabay)
- "Kalau ada kolaborasi, bikin festival atau bikin stand, mau nggak mau aku yang bergerak." (Lusia, Mongabay)
- "My work outside of being an editor, I have a ton of it." (Kresna, PM)

**Design hint from Lusia's Asana quote:** any admin tool that forces contributors to learn a new app will fail. Meet them where they are (WhatsApp, email, a photo of a receipt).

## Five key insights

1. **Editorial judgment.** People decide which issues become stories and which are ready. The review stage holds final accountability. "The editor can reject a story if it is considered unverified."
2. **Workload shift.** AI reduces early work but moves effort to verification later. "It doesn't actually eliminate the work, it just shifts it." (Kresna). AI truly saves time only when it reduces work across the whole workflow.
3. **Editorial transparency.** AI use needs to be disclosed **inside the newsroom**, not only to readers, so editors know what needs a closer look. "If they'd said so from the start, at least we'd be prepared." (Kresna)
4. **Organisational readiness.** Not unwillingness. Limited by capacity, skills, coordination, support. "There's a huge gap in technical knowledge (in AI usage) among journalists." (Kresna). Some newsrooms rely on consultants or overseas teams for tech.
5. **Ecosystem sustainability.** AI supports journalism while weakening the system that sustains it. "We're essentially providing information for AI. It crawls our content, but we get nothing in return." (Evi, PM). "AI depends on journalism, yet it is slowly killing it." (Adi, senior journalist)

## The two implications (my design checklist for the day)

**Implication 1, Editorial accountability: "Can the newsroom trace, verify, and remain accountable for the output?"**
- Keep outputs connected to sources and original context (transcripts to recordings, summaries to documents)
- Show what AI changed and what remains unverified
- Keep decisions about meaning, safety, fairness and publication with humans

**Implication 2, Newsroom capacity: "Can the newsroom adopt, govern, and maintain it?"**
- Fit both reporting modes (regular and extended)
- Reduce **total** workload across roles (don't save the journalist time by adding work for the editor)
- Work with existing support. No dedicated AI team, no heavy onboarding, no maintenance they can't provide

"The goal is not to automate judgment, but to give journalists a stronger basis for making the decision."
